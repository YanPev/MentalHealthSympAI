The model is far better than its accuracy suggests. Top-2 accuracy is 0.72 vs 0.43 top-1, and of the 1003 errors, 51% had the truth as the 2nd choice and 67% are off-by-one — only 29% are genuinely far off. mild(1) recall jumps from 0.45 (top-1) to 0.90 (top-2). The real bottleneck is the final 1-of-4 decision between neighbouring severities — exactly what an ordinal loss targets.

Ordinal loss (CORN) — acting on the near-miss diagnosis

The near-miss result said the errors are ordinal (adjacent severities). So we swapped the 4-way softmax for CORN (rank-consistent ordinal regression) and re-ran the same 5-fold participant CV. CORN should improve MAE — the severity-distance metric that class-weighting had worsened.

Conclusions & next steps

  • Report macro-F1 + MAE, not accuracy. Accuracy rewards the trivial all-zeros predictor (0.436).
  • Retrieval is justified — it helps every condition and keeps inputs inside the 256-token window.
  • Class weighting is the real lever — it revives the severe classes. BERT-base and MentalBERT tie across seeds, so the cheaper base model is the sensible default.
  • Per-item: somatic symptoms (Sleep, Appetite, Tired) are the weak spots — candidates for item-specific evidence or features.
  • The model is "almost right": top-2 accuracy 0.72 vs 0.43 top-1, and 71% of errors are near-misses between adjacent severities.
  • CORN ordinal loss delivers: lower MAE for both models (BERT 0.801→0.769, MentalBERT 0.814→0.738) at equal macro-F1 — the recommended default. It makes errors land on neighbouring severities rather than far off.
  • Next: collapse to clinical bands (0–1 vs 2–3) for a decision-relevant target · multi-seed CV to firm up the small F1 gaps · ordinal metrics (QWK) and patient-level PHQ-8 total scoring.
